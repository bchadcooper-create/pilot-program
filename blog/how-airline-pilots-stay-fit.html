How Airline Pilots Stay Fit on a Four Day Trip

By Chad Cooper, airline captainOct 13, 20268 min read

Key takeaways

  • Staying fit as a pilot comes down to two or three strength sessions a week of 20 to 40 minutes, a lunch bag that replaces terminal food, water at about 0.3 liters per flight hour, and protecting sleep on the layover. None of it needs a home gym or a stable schedule.
  • The job does three things to a body, sitting for hours, broken sleep and food from a kiosk at 10 p.m., and all three are fixable with planning rather than willpower.
  • The medical is the reason. Nobody fails it out of nowhere. The numbers your AME reads move slowly over years, and the habits in this article are what move them the right way.

Your medical is the only performance review that can end your career in a single afternoon. Nobody fails it out of nowhere. It goes slowly, across years of sitting for a living, eating what the terminal sells at 10 p.m., and sleeping on a schedule that moves every week.

That is the honest answer to how airline pilots stay fit. Not a program. A set of small defenses against a job that is quietly built to make you unfit.

I fly the Embraer 175 out of Phoenix. A typical four-day for me is a dozen or more legs, three hotels, two or three 5 a.m. shows, and at least one layover that is too short to do anything with. The advice below is what survives that schedule. Anything that does not survive it is not advice, it is a wish.

What the job does to a body

Three things, and they compound.

Sitting. Eight hours in a seat that was designed around a sightline, not a spine. The Mayo Clinic links prolonged sitting to higher blood pressure, higher blood sugar and a larger waist, which happens to be three of the measurements your AME takes. AOPA's Fit to Fly series makes the same point from the cockpit side: long sitting leaves the body stiff and weak in the places that have to hold you up when you stand.

Sleep. A 5 a.m. show on Tuesday and a 10 p.m. release on Wednesday is not a schedule the body adapts to. It is a schedule the body endures. The FAA's own fatigue brochure says healthy adults do best on eight hours of uninterrupted sleep, and that no degree of experience, motivation, coffee or will power can overcome fatigue. You know what a four-day does to that number.

Food. You are working from airport terminals, hotel breakfast, and whatever is open after 10 p.m. in a city you have never been to. Most nutrition advice assumes a kitchen and a schedule. You have neither.

None of these is dramatic. That is the problem. Each one moves a number a fraction of a point per month, and the medical comes around on schedule to add them up.

The minimum that works

Here is the number that changes the conversation: two or three sessions a week, 20 to 40 minutes each.

That is not a compromise. The research on minimum effective training doses, summarized by Stronger by Science, found that 3 to 6 hard sets per lift per week, taken close to failure, produced meaningful strength gains in 6 to 12 weeks. For general lifters who are not chasing a powerlifting total, 2 to 3 sets of 6 to 12 reps per lift per week was enough to progress.

Three sets of squats, three sets of a hinge, three sets of a row and three sets of a press is 12 hard sets. Spread across two sessions, that is six sets each, and with proper rest that is about 25 minutes a session. Do that twice a week and you are above the floor for progress on every major movement.

The details on how to divide that across a four-day with three different hotel gyms are in the article on the least training that still works. The short version is that the sessions do not have to be long. They have to happen, and they have to be hard.

Captain's note

The most common mistake I see from people training on a layover is rushing the rest. You have 40 minutes before the van, so you cut rest to 45 seconds and try to get it all in. Feels efficient. It is not. Cut exercises if you have to. Never cut the rest.

Plan by layover, not by week

Here is where pilots have an advantage over almost everyone else trying to stay fit. You know your schedule a month in advance. You know which hotel you are in on every night of every trip. You can find out, before you leave home, whether that hotel has a fitness room worth walking to.

So plan by layover.

Night one, 14-hour layover, a hotel with a real dumbbell rack: that is a session. Go heavy by the standards of the room. The hotel gym article covers how to make 30 lb dumbbells into a real squat.

Night two, 10-hour layover, late arrival: that is sleep. Not a session. Sleep.

Night three, 12-hour layover, a hotel with two treadmills and nothing else: that is the hotel room session. Four movements, no equipment, no jumping, 20 minutes.

Two sessions on a four-day, plus one at home on a day off, is three for the week. That is above the minimum. Most pilots who think they cannot stay fit are not short on time. They are short on a plan that was made before they left the house, when the information was available and the decision was easy.

The schedule import in Flight Crew Fitness does this automatically: it reads the duty days and layovers and places the sessions on the nights that can hold them. But a pen and the hotel list works too.

The lunch bag

Training on a four-day is the easy half. Eating is the hard half.

The terminal has one job, which is to sell you food that keeps. That means salt, sugar and fat in a wrapper. The hotel breakfast is pastries and a waffle machine. The place that is open after 10 p.m. is a bar.

The fix is a bag. Not a cooler, a soft lunch bag that fits in your roller. Mine holds four days of what I actually eat on the road: tuna pouches, jerky, nuts, protein bars I have checked the label on, and fruit that survives being sat on. It gets packed the night before the trip and it means the terminal is a choice rather than a default.

Protein is the thing that goes missing first on the road. The bag solves that. Carry enough that you can hit a protein target at every meal without buying anything, and let the airport cover coffee and the occasional real meal when the layover gives you one.

The point is not perfection. The point is that on day three, at 2 p.m., in a terminal you did not plan to be in because of a reroute, the answer to "what do I eat" is already in your bag.

Water: 0.3 liters per flight hour

The flight deck is one of the driest places you will ever spend eight hours. The Flight Safety Foundation puts the humidity at about 3 to 5 percent, drier than most deserts. You lose water through breathing alone at a rate that would surprise you.

Thirst is a late signal. The Flight Safety Foundation notes it does not show up until you have lost about 2 percent of your body weight in water, and that by around 5 percent you are looking at fatigue, nausea and emotional instability. Nobody wants to find out what that does to a CRM conversation on leg four.

Flight Crew Fitness uses 0.3 liters per flight hour as the working number, with a floor on days you are not flying. Six flight hours is about 1.8 liters on top of whatever you drink on the ground. The Flight Safety Foundation's baseline recommendation is about two quarts per 24 hours, and a flying day should sit above that. Coffee counts against you, not for you. The FSF notes that caffeine and alcohol are both diuretics.

The practical version: a bottle in the flight bag, filled before every leg, finished before the next one. The hydration reminders in the app are built around flight hours for the same reason.

Sleep is the session you cannot skip

Everything above assumes you are getting some sleep. If you are not, the training does not stick, the food choices get worse, and the blood pressure number drifts the wrong way.

The FAA's fatigue brochure has a short list that holds up: avoid caffeine and alcohol three to four hours before bed, no hard exercise two to three hours before bed, no heavy meal before bed, and keep naps under 30 minutes to avoid sleep inertia.

The exercise rule matters for layover planning. A session at 10 p.m. before a 5 a.m. show is a bad trade. If the layover only has a late window, do a short easy session or none, and protect the eight hours. The session can move to the next night. The sleep cannot.

This is also why the app runs a Go / Marginal / No-Go toggle off your sleep data. A No-Go morning after a short layover removes the heavy lifting from the session rather than asking you to grind through it on five hours.

The medical is the reason

Everything in this article points at one appointment.

Your AME is going to take your blood pressure, weigh you, and work out your BMI. Seated blood pressure has to be at or under 155 over 95 for all classes. Weight is not a pass or fail item on its own, but it feeds the sleep apnea screening, and that is where a lot of pilots first find out that the number on the scale was never as irrelevant as it looked.

These numbers move slowly, in both directions. The Mayo Clinic's list of what prolonged sitting does to a body, higher blood pressure, higher blood sugar, a bigger waist, is close to the list of things that surface at the medical. The job pushes those numbers one way. Training, food and sleep push them the other.

Nobody fails the medical out of nowhere. The habits in this article, the two or three sessions, the bag, the water, the eight hours, are what keep the numbers moving the right way across the 16 or 20 or 30 years between now and mandatory retirement. None of them is heroic. All of them are specific, and all of them fit inside a four-day.

The web app works today and the iOS app is in App Store review. It was built by a line pilot to do exactly this: take the schedule you already have and make the fitness fit inside it. But you do not need an app to start. You need a lunch bag, a water bottle, two sessions a week, and a plan made before the trip rather than during it.

Questions pilots ask

How do airline pilots stay fit with an irregular schedule?

By setting a low floor and planning by layover rather than by week. Two or three strength sessions of 20 to 40 minutes, placed on the layovers that have a usable gym or a hotel room with floor space, is enough to make progress. The schedule is known a month ahead, so the sessions can be too. Food and water are packed before the trip, not bought in the terminal.

How much exercise does a pilot need per week?

Less than most people assume. Research on minimum effective training doses shows meaningful strength gains from as few as 3 to 6 hard sets per lift per week, which fits in two or three sessions of 20 to 40 minutes. The sessions have to be hard and the rest between sets has to be real, but the total time is small enough to survive an 80-hour month.

How much water should a pilot drink while flying?

Flight Crew Fitness uses about 0.3 liters per flight hour with a floor on no-fly days. The Flight Safety Foundation notes flight deck humidity runs around 3 to 5 percent and recommends about two quarts of water per 24 hours as a baseline. Thirst does not show up until about 2 percent of body weight is gone, so drink on a schedule, not on thirst.

Why does fitness matter for the FAA medical?

Because the medical measures things that respond to training and food. Seated blood pressure, weight and the sleep apnea screening that follows from it are all on the AME's checklist, and all of them move in response to strength training, what you eat and how you sleep. The medical is a slow test. It rewards habits held for years, and it punishes the absence of them the same way.
