Why Pilots Get Dehydrated and How Much Water Actually Fixes It

By Chad Cooper, airline captainOct 13, 20268 min read

Key takeaways

  • A pilot should drink about two quarts of water per 24 hours as a baseline, per the Flight Safety Foundation, and more on flying days. A practical way to scale it is 0.3 L per flight hour on top of a daily floor, which is how Flight Crew Fitness sets the target.
  • Flight deck humidity runs around 3 to 5 percent, drier than most deserts. Thirst does not show up until about 2 percent body weight is lost, and cognition, memory and decision making are already affected at 1 to 2 percent. You are behind before you feel it.
  • Caffeine and alcohol both pull water out. A bottle filled after security and refilled at every gate, plus a plan for the lavatory on short turns, is the whole system.

Here is a normal morning. Alarm at 3:30. Coffee in the room. Second coffee at the gate. The water bottle is empty because it had to be empty to clear security, and the fountain was on the other side of the terminal. First leg, two hours, the flight attendant offers coffee and you take it. Second leg, 90 minutes, a 25 minute turn in between with no time for anything but the walkaround. It is now 11 a.m., you have been awake for seven and a half hours, and the total water intake is zero.

Nothing about that morning feels like a problem. That is what makes it one.

Pilots get dehydrated for reasons that have nothing to do with laziness. The air is drier than a desert, the schedule removes the normal cues, the coffee makes it worse, and thirst shows up late. This article covers what the research says that does to you, how much water actually fixes it, and the logistics of getting that water into a pilot on a four-leg day. I fly the Embraer 175 out of Phoenix, which is a useful place to learn about dry air.

The air at altitude is drier than the desert you took off from

The Flight Safety Foundation's review of dehydration in pilots puts flight deck relative humidity at about 3 to 5 percent. I live in Phoenix. The air outside my house in June is not what anyone would call humid, and the flight deck is drier than that.

Air that dry pulls water out of you through two routes you cannot feel. Every breath out carries moisture that the dry cabin air does not give back. Skin loses water to evaporation faster than it would at sea level humidity. Neither one produces sweat, so there is no visible sign that it is happening. The flight deck is a slow leak that runs for every hour you are at altitude.

The ground does not reset it. The terminal and the hotel are air conditioned, and the next leg is another three hours of the same dry air. On a four-day the leak runs most of the waking hours of four straight days.

You are impaired before you are thirsty

The reason you cannot rely on thirst is timing. The Flight Safety Foundation puts the onset of thirst at about 2 percent loss of body weight through water. For a 90 kg pilot that is nearly two litres gone before the body sends a signal.

The problem is what happens before that signal. AOPA's Air Safety Institute reports that mild dehydration of 1 to 2 percent body weight already affects cognition, memory and decision making. That is the window where you are not thirsty, you feel fine, and the research says your judgment is measurably worse than it was at the hotel. AOPA also makes the point that dehydration can mimic hypoxia, which is a useful thing to remember the next time a headache and a little fog show up on the third leg and you start wondering about the pressurization.

Past 2 percent, the Flight Safety Foundation describes the progression: by around 5 percent, fatigue, nausea and emotional instability. Most line pilots never get there in a day, but 5 percent is not the number to worry about. The number to worry about is the 1 to 2 percent you reach by late morning on the schedule described at the top of this article, because that is where the degradation starts and that is where almost every pilot spends a part of every duty day.

How much water actually fixes it

The Flight Safety Foundation's baseline is about 1.9 litres of water per 24 hours, roughly two quarts. That is the floor for a day on the ground, not a target for a day in the air. The article is explicit that caffeine and alcohol are diuretics and do not count toward it.

The baseline needs to scale with flight time, because the leak runs by the hour. The way Flight Crew Fitness handles it is a simple rule: 0.3 L per flight hour added on top of a daily floor, with the floor still applying on days you do not fly. A reserve day at the crashpad gets the floor. A day with six flight hours gets the floor plus about 1.8 L. A long day with eight block hours gets the floor plus 2.4 L, which is a lot of water and also roughly what the dry air took.

The per-hour rate is a practical scaling rule, not a published physiology constant. What the research supports is the baseline and the direction: more flight time, more water, and coffee does not count. The 0.3 L figure turns that into a number you can act on at the gate. If your own experience says you need more, drink more.

The app ties the target to your imported schedule, so the hydration reminders on a four-leg day are different from the reminders on a day off, without you doing any arithmetic.

The bottle strategy

The single biggest obstacle is that security takes your water away, and most pilots never fully recover from that.

The fix is a bottle with a known volume, carried empty through the checkpoint, filled at the first fountain past it, and then treated like a checklist item at every gate. Most terminals have bottle fillers now. The gate agent's desk area usually has a water source. The galley has water, and the flight attendants will fill a bottle if you ask before boarding rather than during the service.

Use a bottle size that makes the math trivial. A one litre bottle means the daily floor is two fills and each two-hour leg is roughly another half. Filled at the gate, finished by top of descent, filled again at the next gate. On a four-leg day that is five or six fills, which sounds like a lot until you notice it is roughly what the air is taking.

Captain's note

The leg three headache is one of the most common complaints on the line, and it usually gets blamed on the early alarm or the sun through the windscreen. For a lot of pilots the actual cause is coffee on the first leg and nothing else until the sit. Fill the bottle at the gate and finish it before the descent, every leg, for one trip. It costs nothing, and it is the first thing to try before deciding the headache is permanent.

Coffee, the layover beer, and the water they cost you

Caffeine and alcohol are both on the Flight Safety Foundation's list of diuretics, and AOPA repeats the caffeine point. Neither one is forbidden. Both have a cost that you have to pay back in plain water.

The rule I use is to match. One coffee, one comparable amount of water on top of the baseline. The coffee still does its job on the first leg of a 5 a.m. show. It just does not count as hydration, and a day that runs on three coffees and one bottle of water is a day that runs at a deficit.

The layover beer is the same transaction with worse timing. It costs water, and the FAA's fatigue brochure separately says to stop alcohol three to four hours before bed because of what it does to sleep. On the night before an early show, that is two reasons to skip it. The caffeine cutoff article covers the sleep half of the coffee question, which is its own problem on top of the water one.

Bathroom logistics on short turns

Nobody writes about this and everybody thinks about it. A pilot who drinks the recommended water will need the lavatory more often, and a 25 minute turn with a full cabin does not offer many chances.

The answer is to plan it like fuel. Use the lavatory before every leg, during the boarding window, as a fixed step in your routine rather than something you decide to do if you feel like it. On a short turn, go before the walkaround or immediately after, before the first passenger is on board. In cruise on a long leg, use the lavatory when the cabin service is done and the aisle is clear, not at top of descent when it is too late.

Front-load the water. Drink most of the bottle in the first half of the leg, so the body has processed it before the descent and the turn. A bottle finished at top of descent means a lavatory stop during the next boarding, which works. A bottle started at top of descent means a problem at 10,000 feet on the next climb, which does not.

After about a week it stops being a thing you think about and becomes part of the flow, the same as the before-start checklist.

Hydration and the number on the AME's cuff

One more place dehydration shows up: the medical. The FAA's limit for a seated blood pressure reading is 155 systolic over 95 diastolic, for every class of certificate. It is one of the few numbers at the exam that can stop the process in the room.

Hydration is one of several things that can nudge a reading on the day. I am not going to give you a number for it, because I do not have a sourced one. What I can say is that walking into the AME's office dehydrated and caffeinated after a 4 a.m. commute is a poor way to get a reading that represents you. Drink your normal water that morning, skip the extra coffee, and arrive early enough to sit for a few minutes before the cuff goes on. The blood pressure article covers the FAA's protocol and what it takes to move the number over months, which is what actually matters.

If your readings are consistently near the limit, that is a conversation for your AME or your doctor, not for an article. What this article can tell you is that the same bottle habit that fixes the leg-three headache also means you are not adding an unforced variable on medical day.

The whole thing fits on one card. Two quarts a day is the floor, plus about 0.3 L per flight hour. Bottle empty through security, filled at the first fountain and every gate, finished by top of descent. Match every coffee with water and skip the alcohol before an early show. Lavatory before every leg as a fixed step. And do not wait for thirst, because by the time it shows up the research says your judgment has been degraded for an hour or more.

None of that requires a kitchen or a schedule. It requires a one litre bottle and the decision to treat it like a checklist item, the same way the lunch bag and the protein servings are checklist items. Many pilots who try it find the afternoon headache is gone within a week.

Questions pilots ask

How much water should a pilot drink per day?

The Flight Safety Foundation recommends about 1.9 litres, roughly two quarts, of water per 24 hours as a baseline, and notes that caffeine and alcohol do not count toward it because both are diuretics. On a flying day the dry cabin air raises the need. A workable rule is a daily floor of around two quarts plus 0.3 L for each flight hour, which puts a four-leg day at roughly three quarts.

Why do pilots get dehydrated so easily?

Flight deck humidity sits around 3 to 5 percent, according to the Flight Safety Foundation, which is far drier than a typical indoor environment. Water leaves through breathing and skin faster than on the ground, and thirst does not register until about 2 percent of body weight is already lost. Add coffee on the first leg, limited lavatory access on short turns, and a bottle that was empty at security, and most pilots run a deficit all day.

What are the symptoms of dehydration in pilots?

Early dehydration shows up before thirst does. AOPA's Air Safety Institute reports that mild dehydration of 1 to 2 percent of body weight affects cognition, memory and decision making, and that dehydration can mimic hypoxia. The Flight Safety Foundation puts thirst at about 2 percent and fatigue, nausea and emotional instability near 5 percent. Headache and a dry mouth late in the day are common signs that the first legs went without water.

Does coffee count toward a pilot's water intake?

Not in a useful way. Both the Flight Safety Foundation and AOPA list caffeine as a diuretic, meaning it increases water loss. Coffee is not forbidden, and it has a place early in a long duty day. The rule is to match it. For every coffee, drink a comparable amount of plain water on top of your baseline. The same goes for the layover beer, which also costs you sleep in the hours before an early show.
