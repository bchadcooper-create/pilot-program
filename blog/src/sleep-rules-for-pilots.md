Here is a four-day I flew recently. Day one, 5:05 a.m. show, released at 6 p.m. Day two, 1 p.m. show, released at 11:40 p.m. Day three, 5:15 a.m. show again. Day four, noon show, home by 9.

That is not an unusual trip. It is a normal one. And it is a schedule that no sleep advice written for people with a bedtime was designed to handle. Go to bed at the same time every night. Wake with the sun. Do not nap. All of it assumes a life that does not rotate 180 degrees between Tuesday and Wednesday.

Pilots do not get that life. What we get is a short list of rules that hold up when the schedule does not, and most of them are in a brochure the FAA has been publishing for years. This article is that list, with the pilot specifics filled in. I fly the Embraer 175 out of Phoenix and I have been testing these on the line for a long time.

## The FAA list, and why it is the right one

The FAA's Fatigue in Aviation brochure is short, and the sleep section fits on a napkin. Healthy adults do best with eight hours of uninterrupted sleep. Avoid caffeine and alcohol three to four hours before bed. Avoid hard exercise two to three hours before bed. No heavy meal before bed. Keep naps under 30 minutes to avoid sleep inertia.

There is one more line in that brochure worth memorizing: "No degree of experience, motivation, medication, coffee, or will power can overcome fatigue."

Pilots read that as a warning. It is also permission. It means the fix is not trying harder. The fix is arranging the day so the sleep happens, and the five rules above are the arrangement.

The eight hours is the target, not a requirement you failed when the layover is nine hours door to door. On that layover the real number is whatever is left after the van, the check-in and the shower, and the job is to make every minute of it count. That is what the rest of this article is about.

## Why the clock matters more than you feel like it does

A 1997 paper in Nature by Dawson and Reid put fatigue on a scale pilots already understand. After 17 hours awake, measured performance impairment was roughly equivalent to a blood alcohol concentration of 0.05 percent. After 24 hours, roughly 0.10 percent.

Run the numbers on an early show. You wake at 3:30 a.m. for a 5 a.m. report. Seventeen hours later is 8:30 p.m. If the day releases at 6 p.m. you are fine. If the day has a long sit and releases at 10, the last leg is being flown by someone the research puts at 0.05.

You will not feel it the way you would feel a drink. That is the part that makes fatigue dangerous. The impairment shows up in reaction time and judgment before it shows up in how tired you feel. Which is why the strategy for a long day is not "push through." It is the nap.

## The 26 minute nap

In the mid 1990s NASA ran a study with long haul crews and gave half of them a planned 26 minute nap during cruise. The summary from the Sleep Foundation puts the results at up to a 34 percent improvement in performance and up to 54 percent in alertness compared to crews who did not nap.

Twenty-six minutes was not an accident. It is long enough to get real rest and short enough to stay out of deep sleep, which is where sleep inertia comes from. The FAA's circadian brochure translates it into a rule: a 30 minute nap before a long flight, and keep naps to 30 minutes or less. The Sleep Foundation's shift work guidance goes a little shorter at 15 to 20 minutes.

Where this lands on a trip: the long sit in the middle of a 14 hour duty day, the crew room before a late show, the hotel room between a late arrival and an early van. Set an alarm for 25 to 30 minutes. Lie down. The alarm is not negotiable, because a longer nap brings on the sleep inertia the FAA brochure warns about, and the groggy half hour that follows might be the van ride to the airplane.

Captain's note: A lot of pilots think a nap on a long sit looks lazy to the FO. Ask any FO whether they would rather fly the last leg with the version of you that napped or the version that did not. The answer is not in doubt. If your airline has a crew rest area, use it. If it does not, a quiet corner of the crew room with your hat over your eyes is not undignified. Flying tired is.

## The hotel room, fixed in two minutes

Every hotel room is built for a guest who sleeps at night with the drapes half open and the thermostat on 72. You are not that guest. Three fixes, in the order you walk in.

**Thermostat down to about 65 F.** The Sleep Foundation puts the ideal bedroom around 65 F. Most hotel rooms are set well above that when you arrive. Turn it down first so the room has time to cool while you shower.

**Clothespins on the drapes.** ALPA's jet lag guidance makes the point that daylight is the main cue your body clock uses, which means a two inch gap in the blackout drapes at 6 a.m. in a city two time zones east of home is telling your brain the day has started. Two clothespins in the flight bag close the gap. A pants hanger from the closet works too.

**Screens off two to three hours before bed.** Also ALPA. This is the hard one, because the two hours before bed on a layover is when you are checking the next day's weather, the schedule, and whatever everyone else is posting. The weather and the schedule take five minutes. Do them first, then put the phone across the room.

Add the FAA's rule on heavy meals and ALPA's specific version of it, no heavy carbohydrate and fat meal before sleep, and the room is done.

## The crew dinner trade

The crew dinner is the best part of a lot of layovers. The drink that comes with it is the worst trade in aviation.

The FAA puts alcohol in the same sentence as caffeine: none in the three to four hours before bed. The reason is not the hangover. A drink can help you fall asleep faster, and then it degrades the quality of the sleep that follows, which is why the FAA treats it the same way it treats coffee. You sleep, you wake at 3 a.m. for no reason, and the 5 a.m. alarm finds you with six hours on the clock and about four hours of useful sleep.

The trade is this. On a night before a late show, the dinner drink is a reasonable call. The three to four hour window is easy to hit when the alarm is at 10 a.m. On the night before an early show it is not, because dinner ends at 8:30 and the alarm is at 3:30, and the sleep you are protecting is already short.

You do not have to give up the crew dinner. You have to give up the drink on the nights the schedule does not have room for it. Those are the nights it would cost you the most anyway.

## Where training fits around a 5 a.m. show

The FAA says no hard exercise in the two to three hours before bed, and the circadian brochure says exercise regularly but not near bedtime. The question for a pilot is where that leaves the session on a trip that has a 5 a.m. show tomorrow and a 10 p.m. release today.

The honest answer is that some layovers do not have a session in them, and the sleep wins. A session at 9 p.m. before a 3:30 alarm is a bad trade: the FAA rule says it cuts into the sleep you are about to get, and you are about to get very little. On that night you sleep. The session moves.

The layovers that do have a session in them are the ones with daylight to spare. A 2 p.m. arrival and a noon show the next day is a training day, and a long one. A 6 p.m. arrival and a 5 a.m. show is a 20 minute [hotel room session](/blog/hotel-room-workout-no-equipment.html) at 6:30, finished by 7, dinner, in bed by 9. That clears the two to three hour window and still gets the work in.

The article on [how airline pilots stay fit](/blog/how-airline-pilots-stay-fit.html) covers planning sessions by layover rather than by week, and that is the method here. Look at the trip before you leave. Mark the layovers that can hold a session without touching the sleep window. Train on those. On the others, the [hotel gym](/blog/hotel-gym-workout-for-pilots.html) is still there in the morning, but a short night followed by a heavy morning session is also a trade worth thinking about, which is why Flight Crew Fitness runs a Go / Marginal / No-Go toggle off your sleep data and pulls the heavy work on a No-Go morning.

Two more things that fit in the schedule. The FAA circadian brochure says to start shifting bedtime by about an hour a day for a few days before a trip when the trip is going to move your clock. For a block of early shows, that means going to bed earlier on the days off before, not just the night before. And for caffeine, the FAA's three to four hour rule is the floor. The research argues for longer, and the [caffeine cutoff article](/blog/caffeine-cutoff-for-pilots.html) walks through it.

## The whole list on one card

Protect eight hours when the layover allows. When it does not, protect every minute of what is left.

Stop caffeine and alcohol three to four hours before bed, and treat the alcohol rule as absolute before an early show.

Nap 20 to 30 minutes on long sits and before long legs. Set the alarm. Do not negotiate.

Thermostat to 65, drapes clipped, screens off two to three hours before bed, no heavy late meal.

No hard training in the two to three hours before sleep. Move the session or shorten it. Never move the sleep.

None of this is new, and none of it is complicated. The reason it works for pilots is that it does not depend on a stable schedule, which is the one thing a pilot will never have. It depends on a short list that fits in the flight bag next to the clothespins.
