Is There an FAA Weight Limit? What BMI Really Means for Your Medical

By Chad Cooper, airline captainOct 13, 20267 min read

Key takeaways

  • There is no FAA weight limit and no FAA BMI limit for pilots. BMI matters at the medical because it is the first input to the obstructive sleep apnea screening, and untreated OSA is disqualifying.
  • The FAA's own numbers say over 90 percent of people with a BMI of 40 or more have OSA that needs treatment, but up to 30 percent of people with OSA have a BMI under 30. The scale is a screen, not the diagnosis.
  • Treated OSA is certifiable, and weight loss works on both sides of the problem. A 10 percent weight loss cuts the apnea index by about 25 percent, and the same loss moves blood pressure roughly 1 mmHg per kilogram.

Every few months a pilot in a crew room asks the same question in a lowered voice. Is there a weight limit for the medical? Is there a BMI number that fails you?

The short answer is no. The FAA does not set a maximum weight for pilots, and it does not set a maximum BMI. You will not find a line in the AME Guide that says a first class applicant must weigh less than a certain figure. LeftSeat, which exists to walk pilots through FAA certification, says the same thing plainly: no weight limit, no BMI limit.

The longer answer is the reason the question gets asked quietly. BMI does not fail you, but it is the first number your AME uses to decide whether to screen you for obstructive sleep apnea, and untreated sleep apnea is disqualifying. So the scale matters. Just not in the way most pilots think it does.

I fly the Embraer 175 out of Phoenix and I am over 40, which means I am on the scale in an AME's office twice a year. What follows is the FAA's own documents on how that number gets used, plus what actually moves it. It is not medical advice and it does not diagnose anyone. Your AME does that part.

There is no weight limit. Here is what there is instead.

The FAA medical does not have a pass or fail weight. What it has is an obstructive sleep apnea screening protocol, and that protocol starts with BMI.

Obstructive sleep apnea, OSA, is a condition where the airway closes repeatedly during sleep. Each closure is a breathing event, and the number of events per hour is the apnea hypopnea index, or AHI. The FAA cares about it because it wrecks sleep quality in a way the pilot often does not notice, and the result is a crew member who slept eight hours and is still impaired.

The FAA's OSA brochure puts a number on that impairment. Mild to moderate OSA produces performance decrements comparable to a blood alcohol level of 0.06 to 0.08 percent. Nobody would knowingly report for duty at that level. A pilot with untreated OSA who feels fine is, by the FAA's own comparison, doing something close to it every day.

That is why it is disqualifying untreated. And that is why the AME is looking at your weight.

The numbers the FAA uses

The FAA's OSA protocol introduction sheet, the document AMEs work from, gives two statistics that define the whole problem.

Over 90 percent of people with a BMI of 40 or greater have OSA that requires treatment. At that BMI the question is not whether you have it. The AME's job is to get you evaluated.

Up to 30 percent of people with OSA have a BMI under 30. This is the half of the statistic pilots skip. Nearly a third of people with sleep apnea are not obese by the BMI definition. A thin pilot with a thick neck, a small jaw or a family history can have it. The scale is a screen. It is not the diagnosis, and a low BMI is not a clean bill.

The brochure adds the base rates: 20 to 30 percent of adult males and 10 to 15 percent of adult females have OSA. Pilots are mostly male, mostly middle aged, mostly sedentary at work, and mostly sleeping on broken schedules. The crew room is not a low risk population.

What the AME actually looks at

The screening is not a single number. The AME puts together BMI, neck circumference and symptoms, and the combination decides what happens next.

BMI is calculated from the height and weight taken at the exam. At 40 and above, the 90 percent figure means the AME is going to want an evaluation. Below that, it is one input among several.

Neck circumference matters because a large neck is a direct indicator of soft tissue around the airway. Two pilots with the same BMI and different necks are not the same risk.

Symptoms and history are what the AME asks about: loud snoring, a partner who has noticed you stop breathing, waking unrefreshed, daytime sleepiness. You are required to answer truthfully, and the truthful answer is in your interest. A pilot who hides snoring from the AME and later has an incident has a very different conversation than a pilot who reported it and got treated.

Depending on how those three stack up, the AME can issue with no further action, issue and require an evaluation within a set window, or defer. The exact path is the AME's call based on the protocol, which is why this article ends by pointing you at that person.

Captain's note

Most pilots track their weight for years and never once measure their neck. The AME will. Put a soft tape around the neck at the level of the Adam's apple, snug but not tight, and write the number next to your weight and waist. It is the one of the three that most pilots have never seen, and it is the one that tells the AME the most about the airway. Flight Crew Fitness logs weight and waist with the clinical protocol. Add the neck by hand before every medical, because the AME is going to.

Treated OSA is certifiable

This is the part that should take the fear out of the question. The FAA's protocol intro says it directly: untreated OSA is disqualifying, and certification is possible once effective treatment is demonstrated.

Effective treatment for most pilots means CPAP, a machine that holds the airway open with air pressure during sleep. The FAA brochure notes that CPAP is most effective when used six hours or more a night, and modern machines record usage, which is what the FAA looks at to confirm the treatment is working.

So the realistic worst case for a pilot with a high BMI is not losing the medical. It is a sleep study, a diagnosis, a machine in the roller bag, and a certificate that continues with documentation. The pilots who lose the medical are the ones who avoid the evaluation, fly tired for years, and have the problem found by an incident instead of an AME.

What weight loss does for the medical

Here is where the scale stops being a threat and starts being a lever, because weight loss works on both of the numbers the AME writes down.

On sleep apnea. The FAA brochure states that a 10 percent reduction in body weight produces about a 25 percent reduction in AHI. For a 220 pound pilot, that is 22 pounds for a quarter fewer breathing events per hour. That can move a diagnosis from moderate to mild, or in some cases below the treatment threshold entirely. Whether it changes the treatment plan is a decision for the sleep physician, but the direction is not in doubt.

On blood pressure. The same weight loss moves the other number the AME is watching. The Mayo Clinic estimates roughly 1 mmHg of blood pressure per kilogram lost. Ten kilograms is about 10 mmHg off a seated reading that has to stay at or under 155/95 under Item 55. The article on FAA blood pressure limits covers that side in detail.

On BMI itself. A pilot who drops from a BMI of 41 to 36 moves out of the group where OSA is nearly certain and into the group where the AME is weighing other factors. That does not mean the apnea is gone. It means the conversation changes.

The training that produces that kind of change is not complicated and does not need a home gym. The articles on how airline pilots stay fit and the minimum training that works cover the dose: two or three sessions a week, 20 to 40 minutes, built around whatever the hotel gym actually has. The eating side is a lunch bag instead of a terminal. The sleep side is in the sleep rules for pilots.

None of it is fast. A 10 percent weight loss is months, not a trip. But the medical runs on a six-month clock after 40, and six months is enough to show a trend on the scale, on the tape and on the cuff. An AME who sees a pilot moving the right way across two exams is reading a different story than one who sees the same pilot drifting.

What to do with this

If you are a pilot with a high BMI and no symptoms, the useful move is to measure what the AME measures before the AME does. Weight, waist, neck, and a few seated blood pressure readings taken properly. Write them down. Then do the boring work that moves them, and bring the log to the exam.

If you snore, if a partner has watched you stop breathing, if you wake unrefreshed on nights you got eight hours, do not wait for the AME to ask. Talk to your doctor about a sleep study now. A pilot who arrives at the medical already diagnosed and on CPAP with usage data in hand has a routine exam. A pilot who gets caught by the screening has a deferral and a scramble.

And if your BMI is 40 or above, the FAA's own number says the odds of OSA are over 90 percent. That is not a judgment. It is a statistic that tells you the sleep study is coming, and that getting ahead of it is the way to keep the certificate uninterrupted.

The FAA does not have a weight limit. It has a screening protocol built around sleep, and a certification path for pilots who treat what the screening finds. Your AME and your doctor are the ones who apply it to you. This article tells you what they are looking at, so the appointment is a conversation instead of a surprise.

Questions pilots ask

Is there an FAA weight limit for pilots?

No. The FAA does not set a maximum weight or a maximum BMI for any class of medical certificate. What the FAA does is use BMI, neck circumference and reported symptoms to screen for obstructive sleep apnea at the medical exam. Untreated OSA is disqualifying, so a high BMI matters because of where it leads, not because of the number itself.

Does a high BMI disqualify a pilot from a first class medical?

Not by itself. A high BMI moves the AME to screen for obstructive sleep apnea. If the screen points to OSA, the pilot is evaluated, and if OSA is found and treated effectively, the FAA can issue the certificate. The FAA's OSA protocol says certification is possible once effective treatment is shown. The BMI alone does not end anything.

What BMI triggers a sleep apnea evaluation at an FAA medical?

The FAA's protocol intro states that over 90 percent of people with a BMI of 40 or greater have OSA that requires treatment, and that is the group most reliably sent for evaluation. Below that, the AME weighs BMI alongside neck circumference, symptoms and history, so a pilot with a BMI of 32, a large neck and daytime sleepiness can be referred while a pilot with a BMI of 32 and no other flags is not.

Does losing weight get rid of sleep apnea?

It often improves it and sometimes resolves it. The FAA's sleep apnea brochure states that a 10 percent weight loss reduces the apnea hypopnea index, the count of breathing events per hour, by about 25 percent. Whether that is enough to stop treatment is a decision for the sleep physician and the AME, not something to decide on the scale at home.
