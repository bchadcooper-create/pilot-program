An 80-hour month is not 80 hours of work. It is 80 hours of flight time, which means something closer to 200 hours of duty, commuting, van rides, hotel check-ins and sitting in a crew room waiting for an aircraft that is still in Houston.

So when somebody tells you to train five days a week for an hour, the honest answer is no. Not "I should try harder." No. The schedule does not have five hours in it, and pretending it does is how people end up training zero days a week instead of two.

The useful question is the other one. What is the least training that still works? Not the least that feels like you did something. The least that produces measurable progress, in a month where the gym is a different room every night and two of those rooms are closed when you arrive.

There is a real answer, and it is smaller than you think.

## The numbers

Stronger by Science reviewed the research on minimum effective training doses in 2024 and the findings are specific enough to plan around.

For strength: 3 to 6 weekly sets of 1 to 5 reps per lift, taken at 0 to 3 reps in reserve, produced meaningful strength gains in 6 to 12 weeks. That is the dose for somebody who cares about the number on the bar.

For general lifters, which is most pilots: 2 to 3 weekly sets of 6 to 12 reps per lift was enough to make progress.

Read those again, because they are small. Three sets of squats a week. Three sets of a hinge. Three sets of a press, three sets of a row. Twelve hard sets, across the week, and you are at the floor for progress on every movement that matters.

The catch is the phrase "0 to 3 reps in reserve." The sets have to be hard. A set of ten where you could have done eighteen does not count toward the dose. It counts toward the warm-up. The minimum dose is a minimum of effort as well as time, and the effort is the part that does not get smaller.

## Why two days a week is enough

Take the twelve hard sets and split them in half. Six sets on day one, six on day two. Each day is a squat or hinge plus a push or pull, three sets each, and if the room allows it a third movement.

With proper rest, that is 25 to 40 minutes. Twice a week. That is the whole program.

It works because the research is counting sets per week, not sessions per week. The muscle does not know whether it did its three sets on Monday and Thursday or all six on Monday. What it responds to is the number of hard sets it saw before the week ended. Two sessions is simply the smallest number that lets you do that with enough rest to make every set a real one.

It also works because the alternative, for most pilots, is not five days a week. The alternative is nothing, or a treadmill session that was downgraded from a workout because the dumbbell rack stopped at 30. Two hard sessions beat five intentions.

Captain's note: Twenty minutes is the floor, not the goal. Set the floor low enough that you clear it on your worst day. For most of us the worst day is a 9 p.m. hotel arrival after four legs, a closed fitness room and a 5:15 van. Twenty minutes of split squats and push-ups on the room floor clears that bar. A floor of 45 minutes in a real gym gets cleared about six times a year.

## Maintenance is cheaper than progress

There is a second idea that matters for a bad month.

The numbers above are the dose for progress. Holding what you already have is a lower bar than adding to it, which is the practical reason a month where you only manage one session a week is not a reset to zero. It holds the line. I am not going to put a precise number on the maintenance dose because the review I am citing is about gains, not about holding steady, but the direction is not in doubt.

This changes the way you think about a heavy bid month. Say January has you flying 90 hours with three commutes and a recurrent training event. You are not going to get two sessions a week. You might get one. One session a week of six hard sets, three of a lower body movement and three of an upper body movement, is a maintenance dose. February, when the schedule opens up, you go back to two and start progressing again.

What you do not do is skip January entirely because you cannot do it properly. Properly is not the standard. The standard is the floor.

## Spreading the sets across a four-day

Here is where pilots have to think differently from everyone else reading minimum dose research, because the research assumes you go to the same gym with the same equipment every time. You do not.

Plan by layover. You know the hotels a month out. Look up which have a fitness room and which have a room with floor space, and assign the sets to the nights that can hold them.

**A layover with a real dumbbell rack.** This is squat and hinge night. Three hard sets of a deficit dumbbell squat, three sets of a Romanian deadlift with the heaviest pair they have, and if there is a bench, three sets of a row. The [hotel gym article](/blog/hotel-gym-workout-for-pilots.html) covers how to make a rack that stops at 30 into a real working set.

**A layover with nothing.** This is push and single-leg night. Push-ups with your feet on the bed, split squats, single-leg hip hinges, a plank. The [hotel room session](/blog/hotel-room-workout-no-equipment.html) is built for exactly this, no equipment, no jumping, 20 minutes.

**A layover under 10 hours.** Sleep. Not a session. The FAA's fatigue guidance says no hard exercise two to three hours before bed, and on a short layover every hour is within three hours of bed.

**A day off at home.** Whatever you missed. If the trip gave you a squat night and a push night, the day off is a row and a hinge.

Across a four-day plus one day off, that is three sessions and every movement has seen at least three hard sets. That is the dose. It was never going to be the same session three times. The schedule does not allow that, and the research does not require it.

## Do not shrink the rest

The minimum dose is small because every set counts. Every set counts because every set is hard. Every set is hard because you rested long enough to make it hard.

Break that chain and the dose stops working.

In an eight-week study of trained men, Schoenfeld and colleagues found the group resting three minutes between sets gained roughly double the squat strength and muscle thickness of the group resting one minute, on otherwise identical programs. The one-minute group did the same sets. They just did them tired, and tired sets are lighter sets, and lighter sets build less.

So when the van leaves in 35 minutes and you want to fit all three movements in, the answer is not shorter rest. It is two movements. Six hard sets with three minutes between them is a complete session. Nine rushed sets with 45 seconds between them is a conditioning workout that looks like strength training.

This is the one place the minimum dose idea gets misread. People hear "it only takes three sets" and try to do those three sets in six minutes. The sets take three minutes each when you count the rest. That is the actual minimum. It is still small.

## The twenty-minute floor

Everything above compresses into one rule.

Twenty minutes, two movements, three hard sets each, real rest. That is the floor. It fits in a hotel room with no equipment. It fits in a fitness room with 30 lb dumbbells. It fits between the van and the shower on all but the shortest layovers. Clear it twice a week and you are inside the dose the research says produces progress.

Above the floor is where the good months live. A third movement when the room has a bench. A third session when the schedule opens. Heavier sets when the rack cooperates. All of that is progress on top of a base that was already working.

Below the floor is one session a week. That is maintenance, and it is fine for a month.

What is not fine is zero, and zero is what happens when the plan requires an hour, a barbell, and a schedule that does not move. AOPA's Fit to Fly series makes the point that long sitting leaves the body stiff and weak in the places you need. The answer to that is not a perfect program. It is a floor low enough that you clear it in a city you have never been to, in a room you did not choose, at an hour you would rather be asleep.

Eighty hours a month is not a reason to skip training. It is the reason the training has to be this small.
