Sleep and fatigue

Caffeine Cutoff Times for Pilots, Based on the Actual Studies

By Chad Cooper, airline captainOct 13, 20267 min read

Key takeaways

  • The research argues for a caffeine cutoff of at least six hours before bed. In the Drake 2013 study, 400 mg taken six hours before bed cut measured sleep by more than an hour, and the subjects did not notice the loss.
  • The FAA's guidance of no caffeine three to four hours before bed is a floor, not a target. Use it on the nights you cannot do better and aim for six or more on the nights you can.
  • Caffeine works best early in a duty period and in small doses, which is what the FAA circadian brochure and the Sleep Foundation both recommend. Front-load it, then stop, and remember it is a diuretic in a flight deck running 3 to 5 percent humidity.

Walk through any crew room at 4:45 a.m. and you will see the same thing. A line at the coffee machine, and a line of people who are going to drink coffee again at 9, at 1, and once more at the hotel bar because the layover is short and they want to stay up long enough to eat.

Caffeine is the one drug pilots use on duty without a second thought. It works. The FAA recommends it. And almost nobody uses it the way the research says to, because the research says the dose you had at 4 p.m. is still costing you sleep at midnight, and you will not be able to feel it.

This article is what the studies actually say about caffeine and sleep, translated into a cutoff rule you can set by show time. I fly the Embraer 175 out of Phoenix, I drink coffee, and the paper below is the reason this article exists.

The study that should change your cutoff

In 2013 Christopher Drake and colleagues published a study in the Journal of Clinical Sleep Medicine with a simple design. Participants took 400 mg of caffeine, roughly what you would get from a large coffee or two regular ones, at one of three times: right at bedtime, three hours before bed, or six hours before bed. On other nights they took a placebo. Sleep was measured two ways, by a monitor and by the participants' own diaries.

The six hour result is the one that matters. Caffeine taken six hours before bed cut measured total sleep by more than an hour compared to placebo.

The second finding is the one that should bother you. The participants' own ratings of their sleep at the six hour mark did not change. The monitor recorded over an hour less sleep. The sleepers reported a normal night.

That is the whole problem with caffeine and sleep in one sentence. You do not get a signal. The coffee at 4 p.m. does not keep you up staring at the ceiling, so you conclude it did nothing, and the hour it took out of the back half of the night shows up the next afternoon as a vague heaviness you blame on the schedule.

The FAA rule is a floor

The FAA's Fatigue in Aviation brochure says to avoid caffeine and alcohol in the three to four hours before bed. That is good advice and it is the number most pilots have heard.

Drake's data says a dose at six hours out already cost more than an hour of sleep. It is hard to read that result and conclude that three or four hours is where you want to be as a routine. The FAA guidance is not wrong, it is the minimum, written for a pilot who cannot always get six hours between the last coffee and the pillow.

So treat it as two rules. Six hours or more is the target. Three to four hours is the floor you do not go under even on the worst day. On a layover where you land at 9 p.m. and the alarm is at 3:30 a.m., the floor is all you have, and the last coffee is on the airplane, not at the hotel.

The sleep you are protecting is the same sleep covered in the sleep rules for pilots. The caffeine rule is one line on that list, and it is the line with the longest reach.

Use it early, and on purpose

The flip side of a hard cutoff is that caffeine should be doing real work in the hours before it.

The FAA's circadian brochure says to use caffeine strategically during the flight. The Sleep Foundation's shift work guidance puts it as small doses early in the shift. Both are saying the same thing: caffeine is a tool with a start time and a stop time, not a background drip.

For a 5 a.m. show that means the first dose is at the hotel or the crew room before the first leg, when you need it most and when it has the longest runway to clear before bed. A second smaller dose mid-morning if the day is long. Then stop. A day that releases at 6 p.m. with a 10 p.m. bedtime puts the six hour cutoff at 4 p.m., and in practice the last coffee should be the one before the afternoon leg, not after it.

For a late show, say 1 p.m. with a release near midnight, the math shifts but the shape is the same. Caffeine early in the duty day, in the first couple of legs. Nothing after about 6 p.m. if bedtime is midnight or 1 a.m. The hotel bar coffee at 11:30 p.m. that feels harmless because you are wide awake anyway is the one Drake's participants would have sworn did nothing.

Captain's note

The rule most pilots run is no coffee after the last leg. It feels disciplined. Do the arithmetic on a day that releases at 7 p.m. with a 10 p.m. bedtime: the last cup on the ramp at 5:30 is four and a half hours out, inside the FAA floor and well short of the six hours the research wants. A better rule for an early show day is no caffeine after the morning, with a decaf packed for the afternoon leg so the habit has something to hold. Your watch or ring will show the difference within a week, and so will the Go / Marginal / No-Go flag on day three.

The caffeine nap

There is one place caffeine and a nap work together instead of against each other, and it fits inside the FAA's own guidance.

The FAA circadian brochure recommends a nap of 30 minutes or less before a long flight, and the fatigue brochure says naps under 30 minutes avoid sleep inertia. Caffeine takes a while to reach full effect after you drink it. Put those two facts together and you get the caffeine nap: a small dose, then a 20 to 30 minute nap with an alarm, so the nap ends about as the caffeine arrives.

Where it fits: the long sit in the middle of a duty day, the crew room before a late leg, anywhere you have 30 minutes and a chair. Where it does not fit: anywhere within six hours of your planned bedtime, for the reasons above. The caffeine nap is an early-day tool. Used late it is just a coffee you fell asleep next to.

Caffeine is also a diuretic, and the flight deck is a desert

One more thing coffee does that matters more at altitude than on the ground.

The Flight Safety Foundation puts flight deck humidity at about 3 to 5 percent. That is drier than most deserts, and you are breathing it for the length of every leg. The same FSF article notes that caffeine and alcohol are both diuretics, that thirst does not appear until you have already lost about 2 percent of body weight in water, and that around 5 percent you are looking at fatigue, nausea and emotional instability. Its baseline recommendation is about two quarts, roughly 1.9 liters, of water per 24 hours, and a flying day should sit above that.

The point is not to quit coffee. The point is that every cup on the flight deck is working against the water bottle, in an environment that was already working against it. Flight Crew Fitness uses 0.3 liters per flight hour as the hydration target and the reminders run on flight hours for exactly this reason. Match the coffee with water and the diuretic effect is a wash. Drink the coffee and skip the water and the dehydration symptoms the FSF describes look a lot like the fatigue you were drinking the coffee to fight.

A cutoff rule by show time

Here is the whole thing on one card. Work backward from bedtime, not forward from the show.

Figure out bedtime first. The FAA target is eight hours. A 5 a.m. show with a 3:30 alarm means bedtime is 7:30 p.m. for the full eight, and realistically 9 p.m. for six and a half. A 1 p.m. show means bedtime can be midnight.

Subtract six hours. That is your last caffeine. For the early show, that is 1 to 3 p.m., which on most trips means the morning coffee is the day's coffee. For the late show, it is about 6 p.m., which means the airport coffee before the evening legs is the last one.

Never go under four. On the short layover where six is not possible, the FAA's three to four hour floor is the hard limit. Last coffee on the airplane, none at the hotel.

Front-load what you do drink. First dose at or before the first leg. Small, early, on purpose. Match every cup with water.

Pack a decaf. The habit of a hot cup on the afternoon leg is real and does not need to go. Only the caffeine does.

I do not know your schedule, your sensitivity to caffeine, or what else is in your day, and nothing here replaces a conversation with your doctor if you are not sleeping. What I know is what Drake measured: an hour of sleep gone at six hours out, and nobody in the room could feel it. On a four-day, an hour a night is four hours by the last leg, which is most of a night's sleep handed back for a few cups of coffee you did not need. Move the cutoff and take the hour back.

Questions pilots ask

How many hours before bed should a pilot stop drinking caffeine?

At least six, based on the research. A 2013 study in the Journal of Clinical Sleep Medicine found that 400 mg of caffeine taken six hours before bed reduced measured total sleep by more than an hour, while the participants reported no difference. The FAA's fatigue brochure gives three to four hours as its guidance, which is a reasonable minimum on a short layover but not a target.

Does caffeine affect sleep even if you fall asleep fine?

Yes. In the Drake 2013 study, participants who took caffeine six hours before bed lost more than an hour of sleep as measured by a sleep monitor, but their own ratings of how they slept did not change. Falling asleep is not the problem caffeine causes. It shortens and lightens the sleep you get afterward, and you do not feel the difference until the next afternoon.

When should a pilot drink coffee on a duty day?

Early. The FAA's circadian brochure recommends using caffeine strategically during a flight rather than continuously, and the Sleep Foundation's shift work guidance says small doses early in the shift. The practical version is to take most of the day's caffeine in the first few hours of duty and stop well before the end, so it is clear of your system by the time you reach the hotel.

What is a caffeine nap and does it work for pilots?

A caffeine nap is a small dose of caffeine taken right before a short nap of 20 to 30 minutes. The idea is that caffeine takes a while to reach full effect, so the nap finishes as the caffeine arrives. It fits the FAA's advice to keep naps under 30 minutes and use caffeine strategically. It belongs early in a duty day, not in the hotel room before an early show.

Sources

  1. Drake C, et al. Caffeine effects on sleep taken 0, 3, or 6 hours before going to bed. J Clin Sleep Med, 2013. 400 mg of caffeine six hours before bed cut measured total sleep by more than one hour, and participants did not notice the difference.
  2. FAA, Fatigue in Aviation (pilot safety brochure). Avoid caffeine and alcohol three to four hours before bed, naps under 30 minutes, eight hours of uninterrupted sleep.
  3. FAA, Circadian Rhythm Disruption and Flying (pilot safety brochure). Use caffeine strategically during the flight, a 30 minute nap before a long flight, keep naps to 30 minutes or less.
  4. Sleep Foundation, Shift Work Disorder, Coping Strategies. Caffeine in small doses early in the shift, short naps of 15 to 20 minutes.
  5. Flight Safety Foundation, Dehydration Presents Unique Risks for Pilots (2001). Flight deck humidity about 3 to 5 percent, thirst at about 2 percent body weight loss, about two quarts of water per 24 hours, caffeine and alcohol are diuretics.
