Somewhere around leg three of a four-leg day, you stand up from the seat during the turn and your low back reminds you it is there. Not an injury. Just a dull, specific tightness that takes about ten steps up the jet bridge to loosen.

Most pilots treat that as a cost of the job. Some have treated it that way for twenty years, and by now the tightness has an opinion about the hotel bed too.

It is not a cost of the job. It is a cost of what the job does to three specific areas of the body, and all three are fixable. Eight hours in that seat does three things. Hip flexors shorten. Glutes stop firing. Thoracic spine stiffens. The fix is not stretching for an hour. It is hinging, squatting and pulling two or three times a week.

This article covers what the seat does, what you can do in cruise, and the five movements that undo it.

## What eight hours in the seat does

The cockpit seat is designed around a sightline and a set of controls. It is not designed around your spine, and across a career it shows.

**Hip flexors shorten.** The muscles on the front of your hips are held in a shortened position for the entire flight. Tissue adapts to the position it spends time in. After enough hours, the hip flexors stop lengthening fully when you stand, which tips the pelvis forward and asks the low back to arch to compensate. That arch is where the tightness lives.

**Glutes stop firing.** You are sitting on the biggest muscles in your body and asking nothing of them. Over time they stop contributing even when you stand. Walking, climbing stairs, lifting a bag into the overhead: work the glutes should be doing gets handed to the hamstrings and the low back. The low back is not built for that job and it complains.

**Thoracic spine stiffens.** Your upper back rounds toward the panel, the yoke or the sidestick, and the FMS for hours. The thoracic spine, the part behind your ribs, stiffens in that rounded position. When it cannot extend, the movement has to come from somewhere, and the somewhere is the low back and the neck.

AOPA's Fit to Fly series describes the same thing from the cockpit: long sitting leaves the body stiff, and the fix starts with basic bodyweight strength work before asking the body to do anything demanding. The Mayo Clinic adds that prolonged sitting is also linked to higher blood pressure, blood sugar and waist circumference, which means the seat is costing you more than the back.

Three problems, one symptom. Which is why rubbing the symptom does not work for long.

## What to do in cruise

You cannot fix this in the seat, but you can slow it down.

**Hip flexor reset, seated.** Sit tall, push one knee down into the seat cushion while pulling the opposite knee up against your hand for five seconds. Switch. This is not a stretch. It is a reminder to the hip flexors that they have a job beyond being short.

**Glute squeeze.** Squeeze both glutes hard for five seconds, release, repeat ten times. Nobody can see you do it. It keeps the muscle from going fully to sleep during a two-hour leg.

**Thoracic extension against the seat.** Hands behind your head, elbows wide, lift your chest toward the overhead panel and hold for a breath. Five of these every hour or so is enough to interrupt the rounding.

**Stand when able.** On the ground during a long turn, stand up. Walk the jet bridge. If you are on a long cruise segment and the other pilot is fine with it, stand in the flight deck for a minute and reach overhead. The point is to break the eight hours into smaller blocks the body can recover from. The best cruise exercise is the one that gets you out of the seat.

These buy time. They do not fix anything. The fix happens in the hotel gym.

## The five moves

Two or three times a week, with real rest between sets. These fit in a hotel gym with light dumbbells, and most of them fit in a hotel room with nothing.

**1. Hip hinge: Romanian deadlift.** Dumbbells in both hands, soft knees, push the hips back and let the weights slide down the front of your thighs until the hamstrings pull tight. Three seconds down. Stand up by driving the hips forward and squeezing the glutes at the top. Three sets of eight to ten. This is the single most important movement for a pilot's back because it loads the hamstrings and glutes through a long range while the spine stays neutral. If the rack only has 30s, slow the lowering and add a pause at the bottom. If there is no rack, do it on one leg with no weight.

**2. Split squat.** One foot forward, one back, lower the back knee toward the floor over three to four seconds. Three sets of eight to ten per leg. The back leg's hip flexor gets lengthened under load, which is what stretching tries and fails to do, while the front leg's glute does the work. Hold the weights at your sides if you have them. Hold the back of a chair if you need balance.

**3. Row.** Something heavy in one hand, the other hand braced on a bench or the bed. Pull the weight to your hip, not your chest, with a two-second lowering. Three sets of ten to twelve per side. The row trains the muscles that pull the shoulders back and the upper back upright, the opposite of the position you hold in the seat. A resistance band around a door handle works when there is nothing else.

**4. Glute bridge or hip thrust.** Lie on your back, feet flat, drive the hips up until your body is a straight line from knees to shoulders, squeeze for two seconds at the top, lower slowly. Three sets of twelve to fifteen. If that is easy, put your upper back on the bed and a dumbbell across your hips. This is the direct answer to glutes that have stopped firing. It teaches them to work in isolation before you ask them to work in the hinge and squat.

**5. Thoracic extension over a chair.** Sit on the floor with your upper back against the edge of a chair seat, or the edge of the bed, hands behind your head. Let your upper back extend over the edge for three breaths, come back up, repeat eight to ten times. This is the one that is more mobility than strength, and it is here because the thoracic spine needs to move before the row can do its work.

Captain's note: I keep the rest honest on these the same way I do on everything else, with a watch. In an eight-week study, lifters resting three minutes between sets gained roughly double the strength of lifters resting one minute on the same program. Back work is not a different category of training. It is training. Rushed sets are lighter sets, and lighter sets fix less.

Total time for the five moves with rest: about 30 minutes. In a hotel room with no dumbbells, do the single-leg hinge, the split squat, the bridge and the thoracic extension and skip the row until the next hotel. ## Why strengthening beats stretching alone

Every pilot with a sore back has been told to stretch. Most have tried it. Most noticed it helped for about an hour and then the seat put everything back where it was.

Stretching treats the sensation. Tight hip flexors feel better after a stretch because the nervous system briefly allows more range. Nothing about the muscle changed, and nothing about why it was tight changed. You get back in the seat and the clock resets.

Strengthening treats the cause. A split squat lengthens the hip flexor under load, which is a stretch, but it also builds the strength to hold that range. A glute bridge does not just remind the glute to fire. It makes it stronger, so that when you stand up at the gate the glute does its share and the low back does not have to. A row does not just open the chest. It builds the muscles that hold the upper back upright for the two hours before you remember to sit tall.

AOPA's advice to do bodyweight strength work before handling aircraft is the same logic. A body that is strong through its range does not need to be stretched back into position after every leg. It holds position because it can.

None of this means stretching is useless. A couple of minutes of hip flexor stretching after the five moves, when the tissue is warm, is reasonable. It is the supplement, not the plan.

## When to see a doctor

Everything above is for the ordinary stiffness that comes from sitting. Some back pain is not that, and you need to know the difference.

See a doctor, and tell your AME, if the pain travels down one or both legs, if there is numbness, tingling or weakness in a leg or foot, if it started with a specific lift or fall, if it wakes you at night, or if it has not improved after several weeks of consistent training. Those are not patterns a hotel gym fixes.

If you already have a back diagnosis on file with the FAA, talk to your AME before starting anything new. The movements above are ordinary strength training that a healthy adult can do, but your medical file is yours and the person who signs it should know what you are doing.

For everyone else, the plan is this: interrupt the sitting when you can, and two or three times a week, hinge, squat, pull and bridge with real rest between sets. The seat is going to do its three things to you on every leg for the rest of your career. The question is whether you are undoing them on the layover or waiting for the AME to notice.
