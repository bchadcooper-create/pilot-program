Ask ten pilots how much protein they need and you get ten numbers, most of them pulled from a supplement label or a gym friend. Ask the same ten how they would hit that number on a day that starts with a 4 a.m. alarm, includes two legs before the first real meal, and ends in a hotel at 11 p.m., and you get silence.

The second question is the one that matters. The daily target is easy to look up. Hitting it from a flight bag, across a duty day with no kitchen and no predictable meal break, is the actual problem, and it is the one this article is about.

I fly the Embraer 175 out of Phoenix. The numbers below come from Examine's review of the protein research, and the logistics come from a lot of trips spent working out how to make those numbers happen between legs.

## The daily range, in numbers you can use

Examine's summary of the evidence puts the daily protein target for active people at 1.2 to 2.4 g per kg of bodyweight. That is a wide range on purpose. The low end maintains muscle in someone who trains lightly. The high end is for people training hard, losing weight, or both, where extra protein protects muscle while the scale comes down.

Two worked examples, because "per kg" is not how most American pilots think.

A 75 kg pilot, about 165 lb, needs 90 to 180 g per day. Training two or three times a week on a trip and holding weight steady, something around 120 to 135 g is a sensible middle.

A 95 kg pilot, about 210 lb, needs 114 to 228 g per day. Same training, same steady weight, something around 150 to 170 g. If that pilot is also trying to bring the weight down before a medical, the higher end of the range earns its keep, because the goal is to lose fat and keep the muscle that moves the dumbbells.

Pick a number in your range, write it down, and treat it like a fuel load. You are not going to hit it exactly every day. You are going to land close enough that the average across the trip is right.

## Per meal, and why 30 g is not a ceiling

The second number from Examine is the per-meal target: about 0.4 g per kg of bodyweight. For the 75 kg pilot that is 30 g. For the 95 kg pilot it is 38 g. Four servings at that size gets each of them into the daily range.

You have heard that the body can only use 30 g of protein at a time, and that anything beyond that is wasted. Examine lists this as a myth. Almost all the protein you eat is absorbed. What changes with a very large serving is how efficiently it goes toward building muscle in that window, not whether it gets used. A 60 g steak at the crew dinner is not 30 g of protein and 30 g of nothing. It is just a less efficient way to get there than two 30 g meals would have been.

Why this matters for a pilot specifically: your day often has two or three realistic chances to eat, not five. If one of them has to carry 50 g because the next one is six hours away, that is fine. Do not skip the steak because of a number someone read on a forum. Do spread the protein when the day allows it, because spreading it is more efficient and because a 50 g serving two hours before bed runs into the FAA's advice against a heavy meal before sleep.

## Pacing it across a day that starts at 4 a.m.

Here is a real day. Alarm at 3:30, van at 4:30, show at 5:05, three legs with a 90 minute sit in the middle, release at 6 p.m., hotel at 6:45. Fourteen hours door to door. The clock-based advice of "protein every three to four hours" would have you eating at 4, 8, noon, 4 and 8, and you do not control a single one of those times.

Anchor to events instead.

**Before the van.** The first serving goes in at the hotel or the crashpad before you leave. On a 5 a.m. show that is a shaker in the room, or a Greek yogurt from the bag, or two hard boiled eggs from the hotel market the night before. About 30 g.

**After the first leg.** The protein bar or the jerky, during the turn or on the jump to the next gate. This is the serving most pilots miss, because 7:30 a.m. does not feel like a meal time. It is not a meal. It is a serving.

**On the sit.** The real meal. Chicken and rice from the bag, or the bowl from the terminal chain that is in every airport, or a tuna pouch with crackers. Aim for the full 0.4 g per kg here, or more if the next chance is far away.

**After landing.** The second real meal, at the hotel or nearby. This is where the 50 g serving is fine.

**Before sleep.** Lighter. A yogurt or a shake two to three hours before bed, not a plate. The FAA fatigue brochure is specific that a heavy meal before bed costs you sleep, and on a 5 a.m. show night you have none to spare.

Five events, five servings, and not one of them required looking at the clock. The [layover eating article](/blog/eating-healthy-on-layovers.html) covers the rest of the food around those servings.

Captain's note: The serving that changes most pilots' numbers is the one after the first leg. It is easy to assume you are getting plenty of protein because dinner is always a big plate, then log a week and find 20 g before 1 p.m. on early show days and a scramble to fit 120 g into the evening. One jerky bag on the first turn and one shaker on the sit moves the whole day. Nothing about dinner has to change.

## Foods that survive TSA and a hot flight bag

The reason most pilots fall short is not the target. It is that the food they would need is not in the bag, because they packed for a day at home and not for a day in a terminal.

The flight bag standard is simple. It has to pass security, it has to survive sitting in a warm bag for 12 hours, and it has to be eaten in under three minutes standing at a gate.

**Beef jerky.** Dense protein, no refrigeration, indestructible. Read the label, because some jerky is more sugar than meat, and watch the sodium if your [blood pressure](/blog/faa-blood-pressure-limits.html) is a conversation at the medical.

**Tuna or chicken pouches.** Sealed, shelf stable, and they pass TSA because they are not a liquid. Pair with a sleeve of crackers. This is the no-kitchen, nothing-open, 11 p.m. meal.

**Protein powder in a dry shaker.** Two or three servings in a zip bag, shaker bottle empty through security, water from any fountain, which is also where the [day's water](/blog/pilot-dehydration-how-much-water.html) starts. Most products put a scoop somewhere near the per-meal target on its own. This is the serving that fills every gap.

**Hard cheese.** Cheddar, Parmesan, aged Gouda. It tolerates a warm bag for a full duty day better than most people expect, and it turns a pouch and crackers into a meal.

**Nuts.** A partial. Almonds have some protein, but not enough per handful to count as a serving on their own. Treat nuts as a supplement to a protein source, not as the source.

**Greek yogurt and cooked chicken,** only if you carry a soft cooler with a frozen bottle. Both are excellent on day one and the morning of day two, and both are a problem on day three if the cooler has given up.

The gram counts vary by brand, so read the label on the one in your hand and add it up once. You only have to do the arithmetic one time. After that, a jerky bag, two pouches, three scoops and a block of cheese is a known quantity that weighs about a pound and goes through security without a refrigerator.

## Hotel breakfast as a protein event

Hotel breakfast is built around carbs. The protein is there if you go to it first.

Eggs in any form, and more than one. Add the sausage or turkey sausage. Add a Greek yogurt from the cooler if the hotel has one, or the one from your bag if it does not, and you have cleared the per-meal target before the waffle iron is free. If the hotel has cottage cheese, take it. Almost nobody does, and it is one of the better protein sources on the table.

On a 5 a.m. show the breakfast does not exist yet when you leave. That is what the bag is for, and it is why the before-the-van serving is listed first above. On a noon show the breakfast is the best protein opportunity of the day, because you have time and a plate.
